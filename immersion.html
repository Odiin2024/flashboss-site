Inside the course

The two layers English is made of

German Roots and Norman Roots are the pair inside the course. They are the two layers English is stitched from — the one it kept from before 1066, and the one that landed with the Conquest — and they sit between English Advance and English Adept.

They are the reason English has begin and commence, ask and demand. At the end of German Roots you see the seam in a compound word and read the meaning off it. At the end of Norman Roots you read the second layer of English as a layer, not as a list of hard words.

Beyond the course

Past the ceiling, for the curious

Latin Roots and Greek Roots are the pair beyond the course. They come after English Adept, for readers who want the scholarly layer and the vocabulary of the sciences. A hobby for the linguistically curious, and none the worse for it.

Part of the course, and past the point where anyone needs them. At the end of the pair an unfamiliar technical or literary word is not unfamiliar: you take it apart on sight, and the boundaries between European languages have started to look like dialect boundaries.

On every card, in every step

The same card anatomy from the floor to the last Greek root.

The card

  • the headword, in English
  • a definition, in English — never a translation
  • an example sentence
  • a second example — the same word again, or a related word, the cousin or twin that the notes go on to explain
  • a Notes line: part of speech, the parts the word is built from, the etymon
  • synonyms on every card of Adept, German Roots, Norman Roots and Greek Roots; antonyms where a true one exists
  • the British/American spelling split, as a layer — see below

The play

  • a boss fight at the end of every cluster — eight headwords on the grid, answered on the numberpad
  • Fibonacci spaced repetition between the fights
  • a lesson at every cluster door in all four Roots packs
  • three revision drills per pack: the roots rack, antonyms and cloze in German, Latin and Greek Roots; dictation in place of the rack for Norman Roots (its cards are single Old French words, not sums) and for Adept
The immersion law

English, whatever the menus say

Card text is English whatever the interface language. The interface, the cluster names and the Advance lessons are translated into German, Spanish, Japanese, Russian and Chinese. The cards never are — not one card in any of the six packs carries a foreign-language field. That is what the word immersion is doing here.

Every pack carries the British/American spelling split as a layer: a British twin of the headword, both example sentences, the definition and the notes, wherever the British text differs from the American, chosen by your spelling preference or your English voice.
